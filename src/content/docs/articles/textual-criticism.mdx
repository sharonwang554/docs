**Preface**  
In this paper, I am going to discuss the book of Isaiah in the Dead Sea Scrolls (especially the Great Isaiah Scroll) and how it compares to the Masoretic Text (the manuscript that most Bible translations are based on today). The reason for that is because according to online resources, the book of Isaiah is “the best-preserved and the only nearly complete biblical book in the cache of 220 biblical scrolls discovered in Qumran on the northwestern coast of the Dead Sea.”[^1] In the following paragraphs, I will first do a quick introduction on the discovery and importance of the Dead Sea Scrolls and then proceed to discuss a few differences in passages from the book of Isaiah that I personally find fascinating. 

**Brief Introduction to the Dead Sea Scrolls**  
As we know from lectures, the Dead Sea Scrolls were found by shepherds Muhammed edh-Dhib and his cousin Jum’a Muhammed in caves near Khirbet Qumran. It was late 1946 or early 1947, and they were tending their flock on the northwest shore of the Dead Sea. One of them threw a rock into a cave and heard a shattering sound. They later entered the cave and found many large clay jars that contained leather and papyrus scrolls. After archaeologists heard the news, they excavated the nearby caves and found about 900 ancient manuscripts in total. The Dead Sea Scrolls include copies from almost every book of the Old Testament. Among them, the only complete book is the book of Isaiah. It dates back to the first century CE and is currently the oldest Old Testament manuscript we have today.[^2] 

The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls is immensely important. Since it was written around the first century CE, about a thousand years earlier than the Masoretic Text, scholars have gained an invaluable understanding of what the Old Testament was like for Jews at that time, and how that influences their beliefs by studying the Dead Sea Scrolls. They are also really significant for our understanding of the Old Testament. According to the article *“Dead Sea Scrolls” yield “major” questions in Old Testament understanding[^3]*, the Dead Sea Scrolls are astonishingly similar to the standard Hebrew texts 1,000 years later, proving that Jewish scribes were accurate in preserving and transmitting the Masoretic Scriptures.[^4] And from the website DSS English Bible[^5], which contains translations of the Dead Sea Scrolls and comparisons of it to the standard traditional text, we observe that although quite a few differences were introduced through a thousand years of manual transmission, most of the differences are due to minor scribal errors and the different ways of spelling the same word (for example, in English the words ‘honor’ and ‘honour’ are spelled differently but they mean the same thing), which means these differences do not affect the messages of the text. But in the next section of this paper, I am going to delve into those few interesting differences that may affect the meaning and interpretation of the Bible.

**Discrepancies**  
The first passage I am going to talk about is Isaiah 40:7-40:8. These two verses are really special because the Great Isaiah Scroll of the Dead Sea Scrolls preserved both the Septuagint version and the Masoretic version we see today. According to the article *Isaiah 40:7-8 and scribal change[^6]*, on the Great Isaiah Scroll, the first scribe wrote “Grass withers, flowers fade, but the word of our God remains forever.”, and this is the same version preserved in the Septuagint. Later, a second scribe added some extra phrases on the side “Grass withers and flowers fade when the breath of YHWH blows on them. Surely the people are grass. Grass withers and flowers fade, but the word of our God remains forever.” This is the version that the Masoretic Text has.[^7] 

Now, this doesn’t necessarily mean that the second scribe altered the original text. A more probable explanation for this would be that two scribes came from two different Jewish traditions and thus knew two different versions of the same verse. The evidence for that is that the second scribe replaced God’s name with four dots (“tetrapuncta”) while the first scribe was comfortable with writing God’s name. This is an indication that the second scribe came from a Jewish tradition that avoids saying God’s name out loud while the first scribe was from another tradition that didn’t put as much emphasis on the matter.[^8] Modern English translations nowadays like the New International Version (NIV) and the New Revised Standard Version (NRSV) adopted the longer version and translate the extra parts also. 

The second passage that is worth discussing is Isaiah 53:11. This is the super famous poem of the suffering servant. In the Dead Sea Scroll version it says, “מעמל נפשוה יראה אור וישבע”, which means “from the labor of his soul, he will see the light and he will be satisfied.” The Masoretic Text, on the other hand, recorded “מעמל נפשו יראה ישבע”, which means “from the labor of his soul, he will see, he will be satisfied”. The difference between these two versions is that the Masoretic Text does not have the “he will see light” part.[^9]  

But what does this extra part mean? According to the article *Recovering the Resurrection in Isaiah 53: Textual Criticism and Easter[^10]*, “he will see light” is a metaphor that refers to having life. This metaphor appeared in the Book of Job multiple times. Once in Job 3:16 when Job asked why he was not hidden like a miscarried child or like infants who did not see light. The other time is in Job 33:28 when Elihu envisioned God as the one who redeems one’s soul from the pit and one’s life then can see light.[^11] In other words, from a Christian’s point of view, “he will see light” refers directly to the suffering servant’s resurrection since the previous passages in Isaiah 53 mentioned he was buried. And since Christians link the suffering servant to Jesus, needless to say, this is a very important reaffirmation of their belief. Modern English translations like the NIV or NRSV often include the “he will see light” part and add a footnote to tell readers that the Masoretic Text does not have it. 

The third passage that I think is worth noting is Isaiah 9:6. Currently, in most versions of the Bible, it is translated as “For a child has been born for us; authority rests upon his shoulders; and he is named Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace” (NRSV)[^12]. This is also a very well-known verse that is read every Christmas in churches. According to the article *The Great Isaiah Scroll and the Masoretic Text[^13]*, “Mighty God” was written as “אלגבור” (elgibor) in the Dead Sea Scrolls and “אל גבור” (el gibor) in the Masoretic Text. Since the word “אל” (el) means “God” and “גבור” (gibor) means “warrior”, this literally means “God is a warrior” combined. In the Dead Sea Scrolls, these two words were written together, which could mean that it is a name - “Elgibor”[^14]. 

The same thing happened with “Everlasting Father” - “אבי עד” (aviy ad). But this time, the Masoretic Text has them combined while the Dead Sea Scrolls have them separated. In Hebrew, the word “אבי” (aviy) means “father of…” while “עד” (ad) means “again” or “until”. According to the article[^15], “ad” is often used in the phrase “לעלם ועד” (l’olam v’ed). It literally means “to eternity and again” but is often translated as “forever and ever”. The author stated that since “ad” never meant “eternity” and “father of again” or “father of until” doesn’t make sense, this part would be best translated as the name “Aviyad”.

There is also a small difference with the next title, “Prince of Peace”. The Dead Sea Scroll recorded “שר השלום” (sar hashalom) while the Masoretic Text recorded just “שר שלום” (sar shalom). In Hebrew, “שר” (sar) means “ruler”, “ה” (ha) means “the” and “שלום” (shalom) means “peace”. While the difference is just one character, this can lead to different meanings*.* The Masoretic Text version would mean “ruler of peace” but for the Dead Sea Scroll version, it could be either “ruler of the peace” or “ruler of the peaceful one”. The author also suggested that the word “hashalom” could again be a name, perhaps for Jerusalem.[^16] 

And since this entire verse is about the name of the child, the author concluded that a more accurate translation would likely be “Elgibor the father of Ad, ruler of Hashalom.”[^17] In my opinion, the author’s points make sense and his translation is very likely to be closer to the original text. The current English translation, however, is a lot easier for modern readers to understand since it’s telling readers what these names mean literally. Both translations have their value and add to our understanding of the text.

The fourth passage I am going to explore is Isaiah 7:14. This is also a very famous prophecy that has a profound impact on Christianity. The NRSV translates this verse as “Therefore the LORD himself will give you a sign. Look, the young woman is with child and shall bear a son, and shall name him Immanuel (that is God is with us).”[^18] The first difference is that in the Dead Sea Scrolls, the name of God -  יהוה (YHWH) is used while the word LORD -  אדוני (adonai) is used in the Masoretic Text. This does not affect our understanding of the text but it indicates that the scribe of the Dead Sea Scroll and the scribe of the Masoretic Text came from two different Jewish traditions, as in the case of Isaiah  40:7-40:8. 

The second difference is the word “call”. In the Dead Sea Scrolls, it was written as “וקרא” (v’qara), meaning “he will call” while the Masoretic Text had the word “וקראת” (v’qarat), meaning “she will call”.[^19] The Dead Sea Scroll version implies that the LORD will call the child Immanuel whereas the Masoretic Text version implies that the young women is the one who will call the child Immanuel. It is really interesting to see that the translator of the NRSV Bible eluded this inconsistency by saying “shall name”. 

Last but not least, the Dead Sea Scrolls has the name “עמנואל” (Immanuel) written together while in the Masoretic Text, it is separate - “עמנו אל” (imanu el). This is the issue as with the word Elgibor in Isaiah 9:6. The Dead Sea Scrolls emphasized that this word is a name by writing them together and this does not affect the meaning that the passage wants to convey. 

The fifth and final passage I will discuss is Isaiah 61:1. We can see evidence of it in the Dead Sea Scrolls. According to the article *Does Luke 4:18 misquote Isaiah 61:1?*, one of the Dead Sea Scroll copies of the book of Isaiah had “release from darkness” or “opening the eyes” of the prisoners instead of “freedom to prisoners”. Some think this refers to when prisoners are set free from a dark dungeon, they are finally able to see sunlight again, while others (such as the translators of the Septuagint) took it literally as the healing of the blind. But no matter which interpretation you accept, since the Dead Sea Scrolls date back close to the time of Jesus and the Gospel authors, this is a piece of clear evidence that Jesus could have read the version of Isaiah 61:1 with “recovery of sight to the blind” in it and that Luke was not making things up. And this serves as a perfect example of how the Dead Sea Scrolls are able to resolve disputes on textual criticism issues of the Old Testament. 

**Conclusion**  
In summary, the Dead Sea Scrolls date back to the first century CE and have a profound impact on our understanding of the Old Testament. One of the ways scholars studied it is by comparing it to other existing manuscripts such as the Masoretic Text and the Septuagint. They found that most differences were scribal errors and spelling differences that do not affect the meaning of the passages and only a few may affect our interpretation of the original text.

In this paper, we looked into five different examples of these differences. They include different names for God, having longer or shorter versions of the same verse, having an extra “the” in front of a word, and writing two words together to indicate a name. Among these differences, only longer versions of a verse could lead to different interpretations. However, the extra words not only do not contradict the shorter verse but instead can open our eyes to what other meanings the original passage may have.
